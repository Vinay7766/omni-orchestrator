# Pitch Presentation

A pitch succeeds when the audience understands one problem, believes the
evidence, and knows exactly what you are asking them to do. Most weak pitches
fail on one of those three, not on design. This skill builds the deck in that
order: decision first, evidence second, slides last.

The person you are helping may know nothing about presenting or about the
domain. Explain each choice in a sentence so they learn while the deck is
built, and never fill a gap with invented facts.

## Step 1: Pin down the audience and the decision

Before any slide exists, settle these with the user. Ask about anything that
is unclear; don't guess.

- **Who is in the room?** Their role, what they care about, what they already
  know. A product manager, an investor and a grant panel want different things.
- **What decision do you want from them?** "Fund a 3-month prototype", "agree
  to a 30-minute demo", "approve the pilot". If there is no clear ask, the
  pitch has no ending.
- **How long, and in what setting?** Five minutes live, a deck sent by email,
  a judged slot with questions. This sets the slide count.
- **Where will it be sent?** If it's going to a company, check first whether
  that company accepts outside ideas. Many refuse unsolicited ideas for legal
  reasons, and sending one can mean losing control of it. Look for an official
  innovation program, accelerator or submission policy, and tell the user what
  you found. This is not legal advice; say so.

**Slide count guide:** about one slide per minute of speaking, plus an
appendix. A sent-by-email deck can be longer but must make sense without you
there to explain it.

## Step 2: Gather the evidence before the story

List every claim the pitch will rely on, and attach a source to each:

| Claim | Type | Source |
|-------|------|--------|
| "Small clinics lose about 6 hours a week to manual scheduling" | fact | link to survey or study |
| "Clinic managers say double-bookings are their top complaint" | fact | 5 interviews, dated, quotes saved |
| "The market could reach X" | estimate | how it was calculated, with inputs |

Rules that keep the pitch honest:

- **Label every claim** as a fact (with a source), an estimate (with how it was
  worked out), or an assumption (stated as one). Audiences forgive "we don't
  know yet"; they don't forgive numbers that fall apart under one question.
- **No invented numbers.** If there is no source, either find one or say it's
  unknown. Never write a market size, growth rate or user count without a
  traceable origin.
- **Real voices beat adjectives.** One quoted user complaint, with where and
  when it was said, is stronger than "users love it".
- **Prefer a working prototype or demo over a description.** If one exists or
  can be built, plan the pitch around showing it.

If the evidence is thin, tell the user plainly which claims are unsupported
before writing slides. A pitch built on gaps should become a smaller, honest
ask (for example "a 2-week pilot to test this"), not a bigger story.

## Step 3: Build the narrative

Use this order unless the user has a framework they prefer (offer
alternatives such as problem-solution-benefit or a situation-complication-
resolution structure; don't pick silently):

1. **The problem**: who has it, how it hurts, with one concrete example.
2. **The evidence**: why we know the problem is real (Step 2 sources).
3. **The idea**: what it is, in one sentence a non-expert understands.
4. **How it works**: the simplest possible explanation, or the demo.
5. **Why now / why this approach**: what changed, or what others miss.
   Name real alternatives honestly, including "do nothing".
6. **Proof so far**: prototype, pilot, interviews, early users. Small and real
   beats large and imagined.
7. **Risks and what you don't know yet**: two or three, with how you would
   find out. This builds trust; hiding them destroys it at the first question.
8. **The ask**: exactly what you want, from whom, by when, and what they get.

Write the **title of every slide as a full sentence that states its message**
("Clinics lose 6 hours a week to manual scheduling"), not a topic label
("Problem"). If you read only the titles in order, the whole argument should
make sense.

## Step 4: Write the slides

Produce a slide-by-slide outline the user can build in any tool:

```markdown
### Slide 3: Five interviewed clinic managers named double-booking their top problem
- On screen: three short quotes with first name, role, date
- Visual: none needed; the quotes are the content
- Speaker notes: "I spoke to five clinic managers in October. Every one of them..."
- Sources: interviews 2026-10-12 to 2026-10-20 (notes in /research/interviews.md)
```

- **One message per slide.** If a slide needs two titles, split it.
- **Keep on-screen text short**; put the explanation in the speaker notes.
  People read faster than you speak, then stop listening.
- **Every number on a slide shows its source**, in small text on the slide or
  in the appendix with a reference.
- **Charts show one comparison each**, with labelled axes and units.
- **Appendix:** detailed evidence, method for any estimate, full sources,
  team background. It answers questions without crowding the main story.
- **Don't use images, logos, fonts or icons you don't have rights to.**

## Step 5: Check it before it goes out

Run this list with the user:

- [ ] The ask is on the last main slide and is specific (what, who, when)
- [ ] Reading only the slide titles tells the whole story
- [ ] Every fact has a source; every estimate shows its method; every
      assumption is labelled
- [ ] No hype words without evidence ("revolutionary", "huge market",
      "everyone needs this")
- [ ] Risks and unknowns are stated, not hidden
- [ ] It fits the time: rehearse once out loud and time it
- [ ] For a sent deck: it makes sense without a presenter
- [ ] For a company: their submission policy was checked, and the user knows
      the risks of sending an unprotected idea (not legal advice)

## Output

Give the user:

1. A one-paragraph summary: audience, decision sought, core message.
2. The evidence table from Step 2, with gaps clearly marked.
3. The slide-by-slide outline with titles, on-screen content, speaker notes
   and sources.
4. The checklist results, including anything still unresolved.

If the user wants an actual file (PowerPoint, Google Slides, Keynote, PDF or
any other format), build it from the outline with whatever tool or skill is
available for that format; the outline stays the source of truth.
